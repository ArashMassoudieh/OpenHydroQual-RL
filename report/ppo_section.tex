\section{The RL algorithm (PPO)}\label{sec:ppo}
%=====================================================================

We use \emph{Proximal Policy Optimization} (PPO) \cite{schulman2017ppo},
one of the most widely used RL algorithms. It is an actor--critic method
built directly on the policy gradient of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pg}. It is
implemented from scratch in about 150 lines of PyTorch
(\code{train\_ppo.py}), so every step below can be traced in the code.

\subsection{The idea}
PPO repeats one cycle:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Collect experience.} Run the current policy (with its
exploration noise) in the simulator for a while and record every
$(o_t,a_t,r_t)$.
\item \textbf{Score each decision.} Use the critic to estimate each
action's advantage: was it better or worse than expected?
\item \textbf{Improve the policy, carefully.} Change $\theta$ to make
good actions more likely and bad ones less likely, \emph{but not by too
much in one go}.
\item \textbf{Improve the critic.} Fit $V_\phi$ to the returns just
observed, so the next round's advantage estimates are better.
\end{enumerate}
The phrase ``not by too much'' is what distinguishes PPO. A policy-gradient
step uses data collected with the \emph{old} policy, so it is only
trustworthy for policies close to it. A large step can jump to a policy
that behaves very differently, where the old data say nothing, and
performance can collapse. Engineers will recognize the same concern behind
step-size control in Newton's method or the damping term in
Levenberg--Marquardt: trust the local model only within a limited region.

\subsection{The networks}
Two separate small networks are used. Each has two hidden layers of 64
units with $\tanh$ activations.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Actor:} $o_t\mapsto\mu_\theta(o_t)$, the mean of the action.
The action is drawn as $a_t\sim\mathcal N(\mu_\theta(o_t),\sigma^2)$. The
spread $\sigma$ is one more learned parameter, initialized at
$e^{-0.5}\approx0.6$ (on an action range of $[-1,1]$, so exploration starts
wide). The environment clips the action to $[-1,1]$ and maps it to a
release.
\item \textbf{Critic:} $o_t\mapsto V_\phi(o_t)$, the predicted
(discounted) return still to come.
\end{itemize}
For evaluation, the trained actor is used deterministically:
$a_t=\mathrm{clip}(\mu_\theta(o_t))$.

\subsection{Step 2 in detail: generalized advantage estimation}
The TD error of Eq.~\eqref{eq:td} is a one-day look-ahead. It has low
variance, but it is biased when the critic is wrong. The full remaining
return is unbiased but noisy. \emph{Generalized advantage estimation} (GAE)
\cite{schulman2016gae} blends all look-ahead lengths with a geometric
weight $\lambda\in[0,1]$:
\begin{equation}
\hat A_t=\sum_{l\ge0}(\gamma\lambda)^l\,\delta_{t+l},\qquad
\delta_t=r_t+\gamma V_\phi(o_{t+1})(1-d_t)-V_\phi(o_t),
\label{eq:gae}
\end{equation}
where $d_t=1$ on the last day of a season (there is no ``tomorrow'' to
bootstrap from). $\lambda=0$ gives the one-day TD error. $\lambda=1$ gives
the full remaining return minus the critic's prediction. We use
$\lambda=0.95$, so credit for a flood is spread back over roughly the
previous $1/(1-\gamma\lambda)\approx17$ days. The sum is computed in one
backward pass over the recorded data. The critic's regression target is
$\hat R_t=\hat A_t+V_\phi(o_t)$. Before each update the advantages are
standardized (zero mean, unit variance), which only rescales the step.

\subsection{Step 3 in detail: the clipped objective}
After the data are collected, the policy used to collect them is frozen as
$\pi_{\theta_{old}}$. For a candidate $\theta$, the \emph{probability
ratio}
\[
\rho_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid o_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a_t\mid o_t)}
\]
says how much more (or less) likely the new policy is to take the recorded
action. The objective $\mathbb E[\rho_t\hat A_t]$ has the same gradient as
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pg} at $\theta=\theta_{old}$, and it can be maximized with
several gradient steps on the same data. Left alone, though, it would keep
pushing $\rho_t$ for good actions towards infinity. PPO therefore
maximizes the \emph{clipped} objective
\begin{equation}
L^{\mathrm{clip}}(\theta)=\mathbb E_t\Big[\min\big(\rho_t\hat A_t,\;\mathrm{clip}(\rho_t,\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon)\,\hat A_t\big)\Big],\qquad \epsilon=0.2 .
\label{eq:clip}
\end{equation}
Two cases make it concrete. If $\hat A_t>0$ (a good action), the objective
grows with $\rho_t$ until $\rho_t=1.2$ and is flat beyond, so there is no
incentive to make that action more than 20\% more likely in one round. If
$\hat A_t<0$ (a bad action), the objective improves as $\rho_t$ falls, but
only until $\rho_t=0.8$. The $\min$ makes sure clipping can only remove
incentives, never add them. The result is a cheap, approximate trust
region.

The quantity actually minimized by gradient descent combines the actor and
critic losses:
\begin{equation}
L(\theta,\phi)=-L^{\mathrm{clip}}(\theta)+c_v\,\tfrac12\,\mathbb E_t\big[(V_\phi(o_t)-\hat R_t)^2\big],\qquad c_v=0.5 .
\label{eq:ppo_loss}
\end{equation}
The first term improves the policy and the second fits the critic by least
squares.

\subsection{What the two networks learn from}\label{sec:ppo_data}
Readers who know supervised learning usually ask: what are the inputs, and
what are the labels? For PPO the answer differs for the two networks.

\paragraph{Inputs.} Both networks receive \emph{exactly the same input}:
the observation $o_t$ that the environment returns (for the pond, the 9
numbers of Section~\ref{sec:pond}: pond depth, pond tracer concentration,
current inflow, five rain forecasts, and the fraction of the episode left).
Neither network sees the action as an input, nor anything the agent cannot
observe (for the pond, e.g.\ the catch-basin state or the true future
rain). Because the critic is used only during training, it could in
principle be given extra, ``privileged'' information to sharpen its
predictions. That is a known technique, but it is not used here.

\paragraph{The critic: a regression with self-made labels.} The critic
$V_\phi(o_t)$ outputs one number, the expected discounted reward still to
come. Its label is the return estimate built from the rollout,
\[
\hat R_t=\hat A_t+V_{\phi_{old}}(o_t)\;\approx\; r_t+\gamma r_{t+1}+\gamma^2 r_{t+2}+\dots,
\]
with $\hat A_t$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:gae}. In words, the label sums the
rewards that actually followed. Further ahead, the sum is cut off and the
remainder is filled in with the critic's own prediction from when the data
were collected (``bootstrapping''). GAE blends the cut-off over all horizons
with weight $\lambda=0.95$, so in effect about $1/(1-\gamma\lambda)\approx17$
steps of actual rewards are used before the critic's own estimate takes
over. At the end of an episode the future is zero. The critic is then
fitted by least squares, $\tfrac12(V_\phi(o_t)-\hat R_t)^2$, weighted by
$c_v=0.5$ in the loss. When reward normalization is on (the pond), the
rewards in these labels are the normalized, clipped ones.

\paragraph{The actor: no target action, a weighted push instead.} There is
no ``correct'' valve setting to learn from, so the actor has no labels in
the supervised sense. Each training sample instead carries three numbers
saved during the rollout: the action actually taken, $a_t$ (the mean plus
exploration noise); its log-probability under the policy that collected the
data, $\log\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a_t\mid o_t)$; and its advantage $\hat A_t$.
The actor minimizes $-L^{\mathrm{clip}}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:clip}. In effect:
if $\hat A_t>0$, the action turned out better than the critic expected, and
the actor's mean moves toward $a_t$. If $\hat A_t<0$, it moves away. The
change per round is capped at about $\pm20\%$ in action probability. The
exploration spread $\sigma$ is a separate learned parameter, not a network
output, and is adjusted by the same loss.

\paragraph{Where the data live.} All of this is held in memory for one
update only (\code{train\_ppo.py}):
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
quantity & array & shape & used by\\
\midrule
observations $o_t$ & \code{obs\_buf} & $(T{=}256,\,N{=}16,\,\text{obs dim})$ & actor and critic (input)\\
actions $a_t$ & \code{act\_buf} & $(256,16,\text{action dim})$ & actor\\
$\log\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a_t\mid o_t)$ & \code{logp\_buf} & $(256,16)$ & actor (ratio $\rho_t$)\\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Each update, the 16 workers return 256 steps each. The main process
computes $\hat A_t$ and $\hat R_t$ in one backward pass, flattens the
arrays into 4{,}096 samples, trains for 10 passes in minibatches of 256,
and then overwrites everything with the next rollout. Only the final
network weights are saved to disk (\code{results/ppo\_*.pt}).

\paragraph{What about the poor early critic?} At the start the critic's
predictions are poor, so the early labels $\hat R_t$ (partly built from
those predictions) are biased, and the early advantages are noisy. This
does not linger: PPO is \emph{on-policy}, so each batch of data is used for
one round of training and then discarded. Nothing collected with the early
policy and critic is reused later. Each new batch is collected with the
improved actor and labelled with the improved critic, so the bias shrinks
as training proceeds. The $\lambda=0.95$ weighting, which leans mostly on
actual rewards, and the clipped policy update limit the damage meanwhile.
(This is unlike ``off-policy'' methods such as DQN or SAC, which keep a
large replay memory of old experience.)

\paragraph{The exception: behavior cloning.} In the warm start used for
the pond (Section~\ref{sec:ppo}, last subsection), both networks do have
ordinary supervised targets. The inputs are the observations from runs of
the tuned rule. The actor's target is the rule's action (mean squared
error on the actor's mean output). The critic's target is the plain
discounted sum of the rewards the rule actually earned, with no
bootstrapping. This is done once, before PPO starts, and it means the critic
begins with sensible predictions instead of random ones.

\subsection{The full training loop}
\begin{center}
\fbox{\begin{minipage}{0.92\textwidth}
\small
\textbf{PPO as implemented in \code{train\_ppo.py}}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item Initialize actor $\theta$, critic $\phi$, $\log\sigma=-0.5$. Start 16 simulators, each with its own random season.
\item Repeat for 100 updates (single reservoir) or 245 updates (cascade):
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep0pt
\item \emph{Rollout:} each simulator runs 256 days with the current stochastic policy, so 4{,}096 $(o,a,r)$ records per update. A finished season immediately restarts with a new random season.
\item \emph{Advantages:} compute $\delta_t$, $\hat A_t$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:gae}) and $\hat R_t$ for every record; standardize $\hat A_t$.
\item \emph{Optimize:} 10 passes (epochs) over the 4{,}096 records in shuffled minibatches of 256; on each minibatch take one Adam gradient step on $L(\theta,\phi)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:ppo_loss}), with gradient norm clipped at 0.5.
\end{enumerate}
\item Save $\theta$ (the trained policy).
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Parallel rollouts.} The 16 simulators run in separate worker
processes. Each owns its own OHQ instance and working folder. At every
update the main process sends the current network weights to all workers.
Each worker then collects its 256 steps independently and returns them;
when a season ends it starts a new one immediately. Earlier versions used
Gymnasium's vectorized environment, which advances all simulators in
lockstep. That was fine while every step took the same few milliseconds,
but in the stormwater problem rare slow steps (about one hour in 150 takes
about a second) made every step wait for the slowest simulator, cutting
throughput by an order of magnitude.

\paragraph{A performance pitfall.} The first parallel run made almost no
progress. Every worker sat at $\approx370\%$ CPU because OHQ's solver
defaults to \code{n\_threads = 8} (OpenMP), and BLAS/PyTorch add their own
threads. Sixteen workers times eight threads oversubscribed the 32-core
machine. Whenever OHQ models run in parallel, \code{n\_threads} must be 1.
With \code{n\_threads = 1} in OHQ and \code{OMP\_NUM\_THREADS = 1} /
\code{torch.set\_num\_threads(1)} in the workers, throughput rose to
$\approx1{,}200$ environment steps per second.


\subsection{Two optional additions used for the stormwater pond}
\paragraph{Reward normalization.} When most steps cost almost nothing and a
few cost thousands of times more (an hour of pond overflow), the critic's
least-squares fit is dominated by the rare spikes and the advantage
estimates become noisy. With \code{--normalize-rewards}, every reward is
divided by a running estimate of the standard deviation of the discounted
return and clipped to $[-10,10]$. Rescaling all rewards by a constant does
not change which policy is best. Clipping does slightly change the
objective by capping the influence of the most extreme hours, a deliberate
trade of exactness for stable learning.

\paragraph{Starting from a rule: behavior cloning.} PPO normally starts from
a random policy and must discover everything by trial and error. With
\code{--bc-rule}, each worker first runs a given rule-based policy (here
the tuned forecast rule) for a few thousand steps. The actor is then fitted
by ordinary least squares to reproduce the rule's actions, and the critic
to the returns the rule achieved. PPO then starts from a policy that
already behaves like the rule, with a narrower exploration spread
($\sigma=0.3$), and can only improve on it if the data support doing so.
This is a common and practical way to combine engineering knowledge with
RL. The rule encodes what operators already know, and RL looks for
improvements around it.
